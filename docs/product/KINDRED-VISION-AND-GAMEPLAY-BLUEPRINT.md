# Kindred: vision, story, and gameplay blueprint

**Status:** Proposed product direction and replacement narrative. This document is for the team and Claude to use as the source of intent. Revised 22 Oct 2026 after research on game storytelling and child safety, and a check against the current build (sections 2, 3, 4, 5, 7, 9 and 10). It does not describe features that are already shipped. Do not treat the current Grove story, characters, chapter copy, social defaults, or Home mood effects as constraints on this proposal.

## 1. The founder's vision

Kindred is a game for children, initially around ages 7–10, with room for an older 10–12 presentation. It is especially welcoming to children who are reserved, unsure of social cues, or anxious about joining in. They should come for an adventure, creative play, a world they can build, and games worth playing with other people. Underneath that experience, Kindred helps them notice and put words to thoughts and feelings, practise expressing ideas, understand other perspectives, and gradually find opportunities to connect with people online and in their everyday lives.

The intended path is **reflect → rehearse → play alongside → collaborate → connect in real life**, with the child choosing the pace and able to move backward at any time. These are opportunities, not a compulsory ladder or a clinical programme. Kindred should help children use their own voices with other people. There is no enforced point at which they must stop using the app. Success means the app becomes one useful tool among relationships, family, school, hobbies, and community life, rather than the child's primary companion.

Journal can be typed or, when safely built, spoken to a clearly identified AI practice character. A talking animated character may respond by voice or text. The character rehearses situations and helps children find their words; it must not cultivate an exclusive bond. Camera interaction is a separate, later research decision, not a prerequisite for voice practice.

The **daily check-in** needs an immediate purpose: help the child understand their day, choose an adventure suited to how they feel, and optionally reflect on what happened afterward. It should feel like a playful briefing, not a questionnaire or diagnosis. **Home** becomes an appealing map room and quick launch point. **Kingdom** is the player's own buildable island. **Grove** is the lively shared town, shops, and substantial solo/cooperative games. **Ventures** suggests real Singapore activities and, later, carefully organised child-safe group events. Friends can eventually connect kingdoms with mutual bridges and build together.

The age target is a product design goal, not a claim that the current app is ready for children. Child data, AI, multiplayer, and in-person event controls must be completed before a child-facing launch.

## 2. Story premise: *The Thirteenth Route*

Kindred is an archipelago of peculiar islands connected by bridges, ferries, lifts, and routes drawn on an enormous living map. At its centre sits the Grove: a busy indoor-outdoor town where people trade inventions, cook, perform, shop, and argue good-naturedly about the best way to do almost anything. Once, twelve routes linked the islands. On the night of the Great Festival, the routes folded away and the map went blank. No one was hurt, but every resident remembers a different reason it happened. The festival was never finished.

Years later, the player arrives with an unsigned **Wayfinder's Charter**, the deed to a small island, and a compass that points to *unfinished routes* rather than north. The first mystery is simple: who left the charter, and why does the compass recognise the player? The larger mystery emerges slowly: the original mapmaker planned **thirteen** routes, but the thirteenth is missing from every official map.

This is a bright adventure mystery, not an allegory in which sadness breaks the world. The player's mood cannot damage Kindred. Its problems are physical, comic, and solvable: mixed-up deliveries, a stage with missing scenery, an impossible lift, a bridge that ends mid-air, and a mapmaking machine that insists every traveller must take the same road.

### The secret, revealed in stages

The routes did not vanish simply because of the storm everyone talks about. During the Great Festival, the old **Grand Surveyor** machine tried to replace all the different paths with one perfect route. It was efficient and spectacular, but it ignored where individual islanders actually wanted to go. Barbara, the original mapmaker, stopped the machine by folding the routes into separate map pieces. She left clues so a future Wayfinder could rebuild a map with **many possible paths**, including a route leading out of the game world to places in the player's own city.

Barbara is alive. She has been living on an uncharted island, making deliberately terrible prototype bridges and waiting for someone to question the old map. The reveal should be funny and affectionate, not a tragic missing-person twist. The Grand Surveyor is a memorable puzzle antagonist, not a villain to fight. Its logic creates challenges; the player beats it by building a network that allows more than one answer.

The **Thirteenth Route** is the outward-facing route. In fiction, it reveals that the map extends beyond the archipelago. In the product, it gives Ventures its full story meaning and can unlock new themed expeditions; safe family activity suggestions can be visible from Home much earlier. The final story message is: a good map helps you find your way into a larger world; it never tells you that there is only one right way to travel.

### Story rules that make it play like a game

1. **The core verb is finding out where someone wants to go.** A route can only be drawn once the Wayfinder understands where a resident wants to go. Quests are solved by asking a useful question, listening, noticing a cue, and checking understanding. Social practice is the gameplay, not a side task.
2. **One collectible: Route Stamps.** A stamp is earned by finishing a quest or a game, never by journaling or disclosure. Each stamp opens one Kingdom wild section or unjams one Grove door, so the next unlock is always visible (the Mario Power Moon and Kirby Waddle Dee town pattern).
3. **The twelve doors are the twelve Grove shops.** The festival clock stands in the mall atrium under the dome. Each door is a shop that is jammed shut until a stamp unjams it (staff arrive, its game opens). The hidden thirteenth door is Ventures.
4. **The half bridge is the friendship motif.** A bridge cannot be finished from one side. In the Prologue, Maya helps finish the player's half. Later, friend bridges are two players' halves meeting in the middle.
5. **The player has their own arc.** The Charter starts unsigned. In the finale Barbara offers the title of Mapkeeper; the Grand Surveyor wants one name on the map; the player signs it together with the residents they helped and any friends. Full story, voices and scripts live in `docs/story/KINDRED-STORYLINE.md`, which wins on any story detail.
6. **Each act has one question a seven-year-old can repeat** (listed under section 3).
7. **One story, two presentations.** Ages 7–9: toys or in-game figurines, simpler words. Ages 10–12: comic panels, director mode, narrating a game replay, more complex themes.

### Player identity and cast

The player chooses their avatar, name, island name, and style. They are an **apprentice Wayfinder** because they discover and build routes, not because the game diagnoses a difficulty. Their strength is curiosity and choice. They can be quiet, loud, silly, methodical, or change from day to day.

Names are deliberately familiar and characterful rather than fantasy-name generators. The suggested cast can change during writing and playtesting:

| Character | Role and personality | Story function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Barbara** | Older cartographer, brilliant and untidy; insists her worst bridge designs were “research.” | The missing mapmaker and late-game reveal. Her conflicting notes drive the mystery. |
| **Maya** | Practical builder who likes solid plans and hates vague promises. | Guides Kingdom construction; sometimes disagrees with Leo about plans versus speed. |
| **Leo** | Fast-talking festival organiser who starts three projects before finishing one. | Gives the festival a visible goal, comedy, and performance games. |
| **Hana** | Inventor who prefers sketches and objects to long explanations. | Models that communication can take several forms. Leads puzzles and creative tasks. Keeps the festival clock and the Grove lifts running. |
| **Rosie** | Warm, no-nonsense Grove café owner who knows everyone's usual order. | Anchors the town socially and hosts cooperative cooking. Avoid making her an all-knowing confidante. |
| **Nora** | Competitive kid Wayfinder who is sure the Surveyor's one fastest route is always right, and corrects everyone. | Rival who becomes a teammate; offers friendship and disagreement without a forced bond. |
| **The Grand Surveyor** | Overconfident route-planning machine with a fondness for straight lines. | Creates recurring puzzle conflict and the final multi-route challenge. |

Characters need wants, jokes, mistakes, and relationships with each other. They should ask for the player's help with tangible adventures. Avoid therapy language in ordinary story dialogue.

Each everyday resident is also a communication puzzle the player learns to read:

| Resident | Communication style | What the player practises |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Hana** | Draws and points instead of talking | Reading pictures, gestures and objects |
| **Leo** | Talks fast, jumps topics | Keeping track, slowing someone down politely, interrupting kindly |
| **Maya** | Blunt and direct | Disagreeing kindly; not taking bluntness personally |
| **Nora** | Competitive, corrects people | Taking turns, handling being corrected, sharing a win |
| **Rosie** | Reads the room | Watching how she greets, includes and listens (modelling) |

Cast notes:
1. The earlier wizard character (Olaf) is removed because the name belongs to Disney's *Frozen*. His clock and lift role moves to Hana.
2. Keep five everyday residents plus Barbara (late game) and the Surveyor. Every resident is a Blender model, so the cast stays small.
3. Residents live in their own registry, separate from the five playable characters (Kai, Alex, Bella, Tate, Lara), so a player never meets the character they chose as an NPC.

## 3. Campaign structure

> Superseded for story detail by `docs/story/KINDRED-STORYLINE.md` (draft 2): an opening plus four chapters of three doors each, a hidden thirteenth door, no Singlish in story dialogue, and optional resident side stories. The act table below is kept for history.

Main chapters unlock through **completed in-world actions**, not login streaks, mood scores, private disclosures, purchases, friendship counts, or attendance at an in-person event. Every main chapter has a satisfying solo/NPC route. Human collaboration adds alternate scenes, projects, and memories.

| Act | Mystery beat and set piece | Main playable achievement | New world ability |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Prologue: The Half Bridge** | Barbara's patched-up paper map balloon lands the player on a nearly empty island. The bridge stops in mid-air. A sealed charter bears Barbara's initials. | Name the island, choose an avatar, repair one bridge segment with Maya, inspect the first map clue. | Home map room, Kingdom basics, Grove ferry. |
| **I. The Grove With Twelve Doors** | The Grove's festival clock has twelve doors, one per shop, and most are jammed. Hana says the clock is “mostly correct.” | Explore three distinct districts and finish a real mini-game in one. Meet residents through actions. | Shops, games, first decorative build set. |
| **II. The Wrong Deliveries** | Route signs swap overnight. Every resident gives a different account of the old festival. | Solve a delivery puzzle by comparing clues; choose whose lead to follow first. | More districts, clue board, optional guided team play. |
| **III. The Island That Wasn't There** | A route points to an island absent from the official map. Barbara's rough sketches are found there. | Build a vehicle or bridge and solve an environmental puzzle. | New Kingdom terrain/projects and optional friend bridges. |
| **IV. Festival Rehearsal** | The Grand Surveyor wakes and tries to arrange the festival into one flawless sequence. It keeps causing funny bottlenecks. | Prepare a stage, kitchen, and route network; choose the order. | Full cooperative games, festival customisation. |
| **V. The Thirteenth Route** | Barbara reveals why she hid the pieces. The Surveyor proposes one Grand Route; the player designs several. | Finale puzzle combines construction, a game, and a choice. NPCs can fill every role. | Ventures story introduction and an open-ended world map. |
| **Epilogue: A Map That Keeps Growing** | The festival happens in the player's chosen style. A new blank corner appears on the map. | Play, build, revisit friends, discover seasonal chapters when authored. | Ongoing activities without daily pressure. |

**Each act's one question:**
- Prologue: “Who built half a bridge?”
- I: “Why are the clock doors jammed?”
- II: “Who swapped the signs?”
- III: “Where is the island that isn't on the map?”
- IV: “How do we stop the Surveyor's one-road plan?”
- V: “What is the thirteenth route?”

Every finished quest or game gives Route Stamps that unjam Grove doors and open Kingdom wild sections.

The charter, festival clock, contradictory memories, Barbara's sketches, and the Surveyor's increasingly absurd “perfect” solutions are recurring breadcrumbs. Each act should answer one question and create another. Each act also produces a visible change in the world: a bridge, lift, garden, shopfront, stage, or new island edge. No chapter should resolve only through reading dialogue.

### Sample first-session script and beats

1. The paper map balloon bumps down on the island. The player sees their half-built bridge and a distant, colourful Grove across the water.
2. Maya is measuring the gap with string. “Who built half a bridge?” Leo, from off-screen: “An optimist.”
3. The player picks up the blank charter. Their name appears after they choose it. The compass points to a crate of bridge parts, then to the Grove.
4. They choose a first task: fit bridge pieces, decorate a sign, or follow a curious creature carrying a map scrap. Each teaches basic controls and contributes to the same opening.
5. On reaching the Grove, the festival clock whirs. One door (the first shop) clunks open; the other eleven stay jammed until stamps unjam them. A tiny thirteenth tick appears where there should be nothing.
6. Leo announces that the festival preparations can resume. The player is offered three concrete adventures, not a mandatory emotional questionnaire. Home also points out where a family can browse nearby real-world ideas; this does not require finishing the campaign.

After this, Home is the map room with a **Continue Story** card, one optional **Today's Route** card, quick entrances to all worlds, invitations/status, and parent-approved Ventures. Avoid a decorative page that adds an extra click before every activity.

## 4. The repeatable gameplay loop

```text
Enter Home map room
  → choose main quest, side game, building, reflection, or a real-world idea
  → play a meaningful activity
  → receive a world result (clue, material, design option, scene, or shared creation)
  → optionally reflect or rehearse a related conversation
  → choose another route, invite an approved friend, or leave
```

The loop should work without Journal and without multiplayer on any given visit. Reflection gains meaning because it helps the player choose, process, and prepare; it is not a toll paid to access fun. Similarly, Grove games must be enjoyable on their own, not thin wrappers around forced conversation.

### Route Briefing: check-in with a purpose

In the story this is calibrating the compass. An optional briefing of 30 to 60 seconds can appear at Home or in Journal:

1. **Notice:** “What sort of day are you bringing?” Pick an image/word, type, speak, or skip.
2. **Understand:** “What happened?” or a more playful prompt such as “What was today's plot twist?” One question only unless the child chooses more.
3. **Choose:** Offer two or three *different* routes: quiet solo creation, a playful rehearsal, a Grove game, or an offline idea with someone familiar. A rough day may get lower-effort choices but must not be denied fun or social access.
4. **Return, if wanted:** “Did anything surprise you?” The child can keep a note, adjust tomorrow's choice, or close the logbook.

The output is a **choice and a small insight**, not a mood score. No coin bonus for answering intimate questions. If the child writes about serious distress, ordinary gamification pauses and a separate, age-appropriate safety response takes over.

**Feelings Atlas.** Research on emotional granularity links knowing more precise feeling words to better emotion regulation. New feeling words join the child's Atlas when they meet them in the story, never by disclosing anything. The Atlas feeds the 1–2 word My Journey labels.

### Journal as practice, not an endless companion

Three modes share the same private logbook:

- **Tell it:** free writing, drawing/selection where supported, or short push-to-talk voice entry.
- **Try it:** rehearse a specific social moment with a fictional AI character: starting a game, explaining a rule, asking a question, disagreeing kindly, or recovering from a misunderstanding.
- **Take it out:** choose a tiny optional action with a familiar person or approved group. The app asks for no evidence, recording, or proof.

A practice conversation should have a bounded beginning and end, give specific feedback (“Your question gave the other person something to answer”), and offer another phrasing when useful. It must clearly disclose that the character is AI. It should sometimes model an imperfect but respectful reply rather than always agree. It must never claim to be a best friend, ask for secrecy, or create a reward loop for prolonged private chatting.

Practice rules:
1. **Rotate partners.** A rehearsal happens with the resident who fits the skill (disagree with Maya, slow Leo down). There is no single always-available companion.
2. **End sessions inside the story.** After a few turns the resident has somewhere to be (“Café's closing, tell me how it goes!”). No “time's up” screen.
3. **Friendly, not a best friend.** The voice can sound like a friend (the companion tone rule stands). Residents mention their own friends and plans, which shows they are not exclusive.
4. **Every skill climbs one ladder:** try it with a resident, use it in a Grove game with a peer, use it with someone at home or school. The app never asks for proof.

Example chain, adaptable by age: make a short story from toys or in-game figurines (ages 7–9; figurines cover children without toys or who feel too old for them) or a comic, director-mode scene or game-replay narration (ages 10–12); explain the story to an NPC; optionally ask a familiar person to add a twist; optionally bring it into Grove's Story Stage. Typing, speaking, and opting out are valid paths.

### Grove as a real game destination

- **Hearthfire Kitchen (Food Hall):** cooperative cooking with roles, requests, and recoverable mistakes. Solo and NPC teams remain fun.
- **Mystery Market (Grove Grocer or Seasonal Pop-Up):** each player or NPC has partial clues; players can point, choose phrases, or speak where permitted.
- **Bridge Builders (Maker Meadow):** design and test a route together; contributions are visible and reversible.
- **Story Stage (the park):** take turns with a line, image, or action. Older players can use more complex themes.
- **Shops and customisation:** offer identity and playful expression, with predictable prices and no scarcity pressure.

These games provide natural reasons to communicate. Do not award points per message, friend request, stranger approach, or time online. Reward completing a game, trying an idea, helping a team objective, or making something; offer comparable solo routes. There are no public emotional-health rankings. Co-op games unlock *different* rewards (shared bridge decorations, duo outfits), not *more* coins, so playing together has a pull without penalising solo play.

### Kingdom friendships

The personal island is a creative home base, not a wellbeing meter. Building materials come from adventures and games; private disclosures do not make buildings grow. An invited friend can cross a **mutual bridge** to visit, then request permission to contribute to a shared project. Permissions are explicit: view, play, or build together. Either child can close a bridge or leave immediately. Cooperative projects should create a shared result, not let one child overwrite another's work. Plants grow by real days but never wither; a ready harvest waits. Route Stamps open wild sections, so the section split in `kingdom_sections.json` must be frozen before saves record opened sections. Reserve a friend-bridge head at the island edge in `kingdom_island.blend`.

## 5. Social progression and real-world connection

Kindred offers choices along a continuum, never a required social rank:

1. Solo story, building, and NPC games.
2. Optional presence of others without interaction.
3. Preset signals and structured shared games.
4. Approved peer connections and carefully moderated communication.
5. Family/familiar-person activities, curated public outings, and later supervised groups.

A child can always return to solo mode. For the initial child release, prefer small, structured sessions with approved peers over an open social lobby. Any real-time presence, text, voice, or invitations require age assurance, parental controls, moderation, blocking/reporting, and a tested safety operation. Online friends must not be presented as safe merely because they met in the Grove.

Precedents: *Sky: Children of the Light* keeps strangers anonymous and unlocks text chat only after a mutual gesture. Toontown and Club Penguin gave children preset phrases and required a code swapped in real life for free chat. Roblox (January 2026) requires a facial age check for chat, limits chat to adjacent age bands, and requires parental consent for under-9s. Kindred rules:
1. Strangers get preset emotes and phrases only.
2. Free text only between approved friends. Under 13, never free text with strangers.
3. Age bands are designed into the social layer from the start.
4. The existing `SocialLadder` states (SOLO, GHOST, AMBIENT, EMOTE, CHAT, GROUP) map onto this, with CHAT restricted to approved friends and enforced on the server.

**Ventures release order:** (1) curated free or low-cost public Singapore ideas for a child to do with family; (2) monthly organisation-run, age-banded, parent-approved group activities with trained volunteers; (3) only after a separate safety review, invitation-only parent-to-parent plans that at least two guardians explicitly agree to. Public users cannot post open invitations to children. Maps show venues, never live child locations. For hosted activities require guardian approval for each event, daytime/public locations, caregiver attendance, screened/trained organisers, capacity and age limits, check-in/pickup rules, incident handling, and a cancellation plan. No child-only meetup is inferred from an online friendship.

Singpass may authenticate an eligible adult, but it is not itself proof of guardianship or consent for a particular event. Myinfo includes a parent's local birth records and sponsored child records, from which a parent-child relationship can be derived, so it can verify the link for many families; Kindred still needs its own child linking, explicit event approval, and an alternative for families who cannot use Singpass. Singapore's Online Safety Commission (operating since June 2026) accepts reports from parents on behalf of children under 18; the incident process should point families to it.

## 6. Proposed product architecture

This is a **target architecture**, not a claim about the present codebase. Keep game progression separate from sensitive reflections and from child-safety permissions.

```text
Child client (Home / Kingdom / Grove / Journal / Ventures)
  ├─ local presentation and accessibility settings
  ├─ story + quest renderer ← authored content and versioned progress
  ├─ game sessions ← server-validated rewards and economy
  └─ safety-aware social UI ← permissions + moderation decisions

Backend services
  ├─ identity, age band, guardian-child link, consent ledger
  ├─ story/quest state and inventory ledger
  ├─ multiplayer presence, invitations, sessions, block/report tools
  ├─ private journal store and bounded AI practice gateway
  ├─ curated Ventures catalogue and event approval workflow
  └─ safety review, incident response, audit, deletion/export
```

### State and data boundaries

| Domain | Owns | Must not infer or expose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Story progress** | Chapter, unlocked places, clues, completed objectives, choices. | No emotional diagnosis or social-worth score. |
| **Economy/building** | Earned materials, owned items, island layout, collaboration permissions. | No rewards based on private disclosure or social pressure. |
| **Private reflection** | Entries, chosen practice scenario, optional voice transcript and retention choice. | No raw journal text in Grove presence, public profiles, or friend recommendations. |
| **Social safety** | Age band, guardian settings, approved connections, session permissions, reports/blocks. | No automatic chat or location reveal from proximity. |
| **Ventures** | Verified venue listing, event age band/capacity, guardian approval and attendance workflow. | No child live location or unapproved host access. |

Use server-side checks for identity, consent, rewards, building rights, event joining, and communication permissions. A client-only toggle is not a safety boundary. Store the least sensitive information needed, set retention/deletion rules for text and voice, and make parent and child views understandable. Parents need participation and approval controls; the treatment of a child's private reflections needs an explicit, transparent policy with exceptions for safety escalation rather than an unannounced full transcript feed.

### One quest end-to-end

1. The story engine selects an authored quest using chapter progress and the player's choice, not a hidden emotional label.
2. The client displays the quest and available solo/NPC/approved-peer modes.
3. The child plays. The game service validates the objective and records only the necessary result.
4. The reward service grants a clue/material/design choice once, with duplicate protection.
5. The world state updates and Home shows the next meaningful route.
6. Journal may offer an optional related reflection or rehearsal. Its content remains in the private domain.
7. If the quest suggests a real-world activity, the parent-facing workflow handles approval separately.

### One social invitation end-to-end

Guardian settings and the child's own choice must permit the feature → the child sends an in-app invitation to an approved peer → recipient chooses to accept → a scoped game session opens with only its allowed communication tools → both have instant exit/block/report → the session ends and only necessary progress is retained. A Kingdom bridge requires a separate mutual permission. A Venture requires a separate guardian approval for that particular activity.

## 7. Safeguards and research decisions

- **Voice before camera.** Push-to-talk, short sessions, clear recording indicators, transcript review where useful, and data-minimising retention are a more proportionate first experiment. Camera can reveal faces, homes, and bystanders; it needs a distinct privacy and child-safety review. Do not score eye contact, facial emotion, or a single “correct” social style. If camera is ever added, the safest first use is on-device face tracking that animates the player's avatar (like Memoji): expression practice with no video leaving the device.
- **AI is a practice tool.** Clearly label it, bound interactions, test child-specific harmful responses, and have age-appropriate escalation to trusted adults/support when needed. Human relationships and professional care are outside the AI's role. AI companions for minors are under heavy scrutiny (Character.AI ended open-ended chat for under-18s in late 2025; a US Senate bill proposes banning AI companions for minors), so the practice-tool framing is a requirement.
- **Safety before social launch.** Validate account age/guardian links, moderation, reports, emergency workflow, operational staffing, and privacy before enabling open interaction. Existing adult-oriented or generic safety functions are not proof of child readiness.
- **No manipulative retention.** Avoid streak losses, timed scarcity, guilt about absence, and a sky or island that deteriorates because a child reports feeling bad. Returning after a long break should be easy.
- **Accessibility and inclusion.** Text and voice choices, visual/nonverbal participation, reading support, controllable sound, and no assumption that looking away or speaking less means poor social skill.
- **Research honestly.** Test whether children enjoy the game and whether optional skills carry into real interactions. Do not claim a clinical treatment effect from usage or engagement metrics.

## 8. Delivery pipeline and decision gates

The order protects the story and the children who would use it. Each phase should produce a playable or reviewable artifact before the next one expands risk.

| Phase | Build | Exit check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **0. Story prototype** | Script the first 20–30 minutes, chapter outline, character sheets, clue board, first Grove game, and Home map room wireflow. | Children and caregivers understand the mystery and want to play without wellbeing framing. |
| **1. Solo vertical slice** | First island, half bridge, Grove hub, one polished game with NPCs, build reward, optional Route Briefing. | A complete enjoyable loop works with no journal disclosure or multiplayer. |
| **2. Private expression** | Typed logbook, specific conversation rehearsals, child-tested AI safety, server-side model gateway, privacy controls. Pilot voice only after separate review. | Practice is understandable, bounded, useful, and has tested safety responses. |
| **3. Structured peer play** | Approved friend links, small cooperative game sessions, instant exit, moderation and reporting operations, bridge permissions. | Safety team can detect/respond to problems; child can leave and recover easily. |
| **4. Real-world discovery** | Curated venue/activity catalogue, parent-facing suggestions, accessibility and cost details. | Listings are accurate, family-friendly, and show no child location. |
| **5. Hosted Ventures** | Organisation-run events, adult identity/guardian linkage, event approvals, staff screening/training, incident workflow. | A formal child-safety and privacy review signs off before any event is offered. |
| **6. Expanded campaign** | Remaining acts, seasonal mysteries, more games, optional parent-to-parent invitation design. | New features preserve solo completion and do not turn socialising into a score. |

Useful evaluation measures: quest comprehension and enjoyment; voluntary return without streak pressure; completion of solo and cooperative games; whether children can describe or try a communication strategy with someone they know; child and caregiver reported comfort; rate and resolution of safety incidents. Do not optimise for chat minutes, number of friends, emotional disclosures, or the speed at which a child reaches real-world events.
